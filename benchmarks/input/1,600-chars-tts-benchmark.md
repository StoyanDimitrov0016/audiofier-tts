At 7:45 a.m. on Tuesday, Dr. Elena Morris opened the lab and said, “Good morning. Today we test clarity, rhythm, pronunciation and natural pacing.” The room was quiet except for a fan, a clock and rain tapping against the window. She read a list: coffee, water, aluminum, February, library, rural, mirror, comfortable, temperature, and particularly.

Next came numbers. The sensor reported 0.7 volts, 12.5 percent humidity change, 3,409 samples, and a peak frequency of 22.05 kilohertz. A package weighed 1.25 kilograms and cost $49.99. The reference code was A-17-B, version 2.4.1, build 8063. The date was August 25, 2026, and the estimated arrival time was 14:30.

“Can you hear the difference,” she asked, “between fifteen and fifty, thirteen and thirty, or sixty and sixteen?” Then she tried abbreviations: CPU, GPU, API, HTTP, JSON, SQL, NATO, NASA, and U.S.A. She continued with names and places: Sofia, London, Zürich, São Paulo, Kyoto, Nguyễn, José, Chloë, and O’Connor.

For expression, she changed tone. “Wait—what happened?” A moment later: “Excellent! That worked better than expected.” Then, more calmly, “Please save the file, close the window, and restart the service.” She whispered, “This sentence should sound soft,” then declared, “This one should sound confident and clear.”

Finally, she tested difficult transitions: crisp spring streets, sixth street lamps, fresh shrimp, red leather, unique New York, quick brown foxes, and three free throws. The benchmark ended with a simple question: Does the voice sound human, consistent, intelligible and pleasant across every sentence?
